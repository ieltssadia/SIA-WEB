Section 4. You will hear a lecture on a very popular Canadian food product — maple syrup.

Maple syrup can be described as a very sweet Canadian treat, poured generously over things like pancakes for breakfast. The syrup is produced from the maple tree, and it actually comes from the tree's sap — the watery substance that comes out of the trunk of the tree when it is cut. In order for enough sap to be produced, very special climate conditions are needed: in spring, the temperatures need to drop to well below zero at night, but then they need to rise sharply during the day, 31so they're hugely different over a 24-hour period — and that's exactly what happens in north-eastern Canada every spring.

The indigenous peoples of North America, known as the First Nations, discovered the maple secret long before the Europeans arrived. Early folk stories say it was first discovered by a chief who threw his tomahawk, which is similar to an axe, into the trunk of a tree. Clear sap started to run from the cut, and 32his wife used this sap to boil meat, and the heat turned it into a sweet syrup which they found delicious. Whether or not that story is true, we do know that the maple tree was very important to First Nations peoples for hundreds of years. 33Collecting the sap each spring became a tradition among them — whole families would migrate to the areas where the maple trees grew and set up camp there.

They collected the sap from the trees using buckets. Then they needed to concentrate the sap, removing some of the water from it, to create syrup. They had two ways of doing this. 34One way was to drop hot stones from a fire into the buckets to create steam — repeated often enough, this would eventually concentrate the syrup. Another method was to leave the sap outside overnight, exposed to freezing temperatures. 35This would mean that a thick layer of ice would form on top, which could be discarded, leaving the sugary sap below. Again, if you repeat it over several days, eventually a sweet brown syrup was produced.

The First Nations people had various uses for the sap and syrup. They drank the sap fresh from the tree as a tonic, knowing that it was an important source of energy. But for the most part they reduced the syrup further and then cooled it so that it crystallised into sugar — this was easy to carry and kept indefinitely. European settlers who arrived in Canada began to learn about the syrup from First Nations peoples and further expanded its uses: for example, 36they incorporated it into medicine of different kinds. They also discovered that by adding yeast to the sap they could make a maple drink, and by throwing the hot syrup onto the snow they could make a toffee-like candy.

But like the First Nations, they turned most of their sap into maple sugar. For most people it was the only type of sugar they could afford, since the only alternative was the sugar imported from the Caribbean, which was made from sugarcane, and this was very expensive. 37However, after 1880 the Canadian government decided to stop charging tax on Caribbean sugar, and this made it much cheaper. After that, it was no longer cost-effective to produce maple sugar, and syrup-making became the only viable industry remaining for Canadians — and that's what we've been doing ever since.

At present, maple syrup is enjoying a boom — in the last two decades production has doubled in Canada. However, serious threats remain. Like any agricultural product, 38the harvest is subject to such things as major storms or the arrival of invasive insects — both of which have happened — and these could destroy everything. It is predicted with some certainty that in the longer term climate change will also have an adverse effect on the sap run. Then there are the social factors: producing maple syrup is a very labour-intensive process, making it an expensive purchase, and 39of equal concern is the fact that nowadays there's a lot of competition from cheaper, artificially flavoured brands. Furthermore, we're at a time when obesity is a major issue, making a product that is 67% sugar increasingly difficult to sell.

Nevertheless, there are reasons to be optimistic. Producers are developing markets abroad — our syrup is currently being exported to over 50 countries. It is high in calories and antioxidants; 40as such, it is still relatively healthy when you compare it with sugar or corn syrup. Canadians are proud of maple syrup and its deep roots in our history and geography. However, the primary reason for maple syrup's success around the world remains its taste — there's nothing quite like it.
