SECTION 4 You will hear part of a lecture for teacher trainees on using drama activities in the classroom. First you have some time to look at questions 31 to 40 on page eight. Now listen carefully and answer questions 31 to 40. Lecturer: We’re going to look at why it’s beneficial to include drama activities (such as role-play) in teaching across subjects. There’s a lot of evidence that drama helps personal development. Creating space for children to express themselves freely in groups helps give them confidence in their opinions and ideas—works especially well for shy or introverted children. Unscripted improvisation lets children take risks safely and say things they might normally be afraid to say. Drama also supports educational development. Emphasis on cooperation means group members must learn the value of listening. Being in performances that rely on everyone working together helps children be responsible—for learning and for general behavior. Now, to role-play as a learning tool. Its therapeutic value is widely accepted. Acting out aspects of their own life (without stating it’s direct) helps children judge their own behavior objectively—for example, a bully doing a role-reversal as a victim. Role-play lets teachers explore taboo or controversial subjects in a safe setting where children feel at ease—no criticism for voicing views. Examining thoughts can deepen self-knowledge and strengthen morality—important for social skills. For history, drama brings characters from the past to life, improving participation and helping children remember what happened and why it mattered. Asking children to solve problems via role-play (e.g., how to defend a town or where to build a settlement) focuses attention on decision-making. Okay—think about your next teaching practice; in groups discuss how you’d incorporate an element of drama into your lesson plan. That is the end of section four.
